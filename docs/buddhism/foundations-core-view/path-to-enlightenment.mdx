## The Three Trainings

The Buddha summarized the path to liberation in three essential disciplines.
They are like the legs of a stool - remove one and the others cannot bear
the weight alone.

### **1. Ethical Conduct (Sīla)**

Living in a way that avoids harm and supports clarity of mind. Ethics are
not a list of prohibitions. They are the way we arrange our speech, our
actions, and our livelihood so that the heart has room to settle.

A life lived with integrity creates the foundation for stable meditation and
wholesome intentions.

### **2. Meditation (Samādhi)**

Training the mind to become calm, focused, and steady. In meditation we learn
to place attention where we choose, and to notice - without being swept away -
when the mind wanders.

A concentrated mind can see reality clearly and respond wisely. It does not
react to every passing mood as if the mood were the whole truth.

### **3. Wisdom (Paññā)**

Understanding the true nature of existence - impermanence, suffering, and
not-self. Wisdom is not book knowledge. It is the direct seeing of how things
are.

Wisdom cuts through ignorance, which the teachings name as the root of all
suffering.

The three trainings support one another. Ethics stabilizes the mind. Meditation
deepens clarity. Wisdom transforms understanding into freedom.

## The Six Perfections

For those following the bodhisattva path, the **Six Perfections** (Pāramitās)
describe the qualities that lead to full awakening. Each one is a practice,
not a possession.

- **Generosity (Dāna):** giving freely, reducing attachment, and opening the
  heart. Generosity begins with the small willingness to offer what we have.

- **Ethical Conduct (Sīla):** acting with integrity and kindness. Holding
  ourselves to a clean standard even when no one is watching.

- **Patience (Kṣānti):** meeting difficulties with calm and understanding.
  The willingness to stay present when the situation asks more than we
  expected.

- **Joyful Effort (Vīrya):** sustaining energy and enthusiasm for practice.
  Not the grim effort of forcing, but the glad effort of returning to the path
  each morning.

- **Meditative Concentration (Dhyāna):** developing deep stability and clarity
  of mind. The still point from which wisdom can arise.

- **Wisdom (Prajñā):** seeing reality as it truly is. The fruit of the other
  five, and the heart of the path.

Practiced together, these perfections gradually transform the mind and prepare
it for full awakening.

## Stages of the Path

The Buddhist path unfolds gradually, through understanding and direct
experience. The stages below are landmarks, not signposts that demand a
particular speed.

### **1. Recognizing Suffering**

Seeing clearly that life contains stress, dissatisfaction, and instability.
Not as a judgment, but as a fact to be observed.

### **2. Understanding the Causes**

Realizing that craving, ignorance, and unskillful habits create suffering.
Tracing the chain back to its roots.

### **3. Cultivating the Path**

Practicing ethics, meditation, and wisdom to weaken the causes of suffering.
This is the long, steady work.

### **4. Experiencing Insight**

Seeing impermanence, not-self, and dependent origination directly. The view
that becomes experience rather than concept.

### **5. Letting Go**

Releasing attachment, craving, and the illusions that bind us to samsara.
A gentle unclenching of the hands.

### **6. Liberation**

The end of suffering - nibbāna - a state of profound peace, clarity, and
freedom.

## Walking the Path

The path to enlightenment is not about belief. It is about practice.

Each moment offers a chance to cultivate kindness, clarity, and understanding.
Each small choice, repeated, becomes the direction of a life.

Progress is gradual, shaped by:

- consistent meditation,
- ethical living,
- mindful awareness,
- and sincere effort.

With patience and dedication, the path becomes clearer. The mind moves steadily
toward liberation - not by rushing forward, but by walking, one step at a time,
in the direction of freedom.
