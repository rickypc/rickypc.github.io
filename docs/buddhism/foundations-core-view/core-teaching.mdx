## The Four Noble Truths

The Buddha sat beneath the Bodhi tree and, in a single night of clear seeing,
summarized the entire path in four insights.

These are not doctrines to argue. They are observations, the way a doctor
observes a sickness and its remedy.

- **There is suffering (dukkha):** life contains stress, dissatisfaction, and
  instability. Birth is difficult. Aging is difficult. Loss is difficult. Even
  pleasant things pass, and their passing is difficult.

- **Suffering has causes:** craving, ignorance, and unskillful habits keep the
  pattern turning. We reach for what is pleasant, push away what is unpleasant,
  and grow numb to what is neutral - and each reach tightens the knot.

- **Suffering can end:** when its causes are removed, freedom becomes possible.
  The knot is not part of the rope. It was tied by hands, and hands can untie
  it.

- **There is a path to the end of suffering:** the Noble Eightfold Path leads
  to clarity, peace, and liberation. It is not a belief to hold; it is a way
  to walk.

When these four are seen clearly, the heart already begins to soften.

## The Three Marks of Existence

Three characteristics belong to every conditioned thing. To see them is to
see the nature of what is.

- **Impermanence (anicca):** everything changes. The breath moves in, the
  breath moves out. The seasons turn. The cup on the table will not be there
  in fifty years. Nothing we love is permanent, and nothing we fear is
  permanent either.

- **Suffering or unsatisfactoriness (dukkha):** clinging to what changes
  creates stress. We hold the pleasant moment and it slips; we push the
  unpleasant moment and it lingers.

- **Not-self (anattā):** no permanent, unchanging "self" can be found in body
  or mind. What we call "me" is a process - a stream of sensations, thoughts,
  feelings, and choices that arises and passes from moment to moment.

Seeing these three clearly loosens the grip of attachment and opens the door
to wisdom. The world does not become less beautiful. It becomes more honest.

## Dependent Origination

Dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) is the teaching that nothing exists
independently. Everything arises from conditions; everything depends on
countless other things.

This is not a philosophical abstraction. It is something we can notice in any
ordinary moment. The mood this morning follows the sleep we had, which followed
the day before, which followed the meal before that. A conversation in the
afternoon shifts the evening, which shifts the night. Each moment is the
meeting of many causes.

The teaching shows:

- why we are reborn,
- how habits shape our lives,
- and how liberation becomes possible when the chain is broken.

Understanding dependent origination reveals the mechanics of samsara - and, at
the same time, the path that leads out of it.

## Karma & Rebirth

Karma is intentional action. Every thought, word, and deed leaves an imprint
on the mind. These imprints shape what we will experience next, including the
circumstances of our next life.

A few key points, kept simple:

- Good intentions strengthen wholesome qualities.
- Harmful intentions strengthen unwholesome qualities.
- Karma ripens when conditions allow it, sometimes quickly, sometimes after a
  long time.
- Rebirth continues as long as craving and ignorance persist.

Human birth arises from a mix of past actions and present conditions. It
offers a rare chance to grow and to resolve old karmic patterns - which is
why so many of the teachings center on how to use this particular life well.

## Bodhicitta

Bodhicitta is the awakened heart - the intention to attain enlightenment for
the benefit of all beings.

It is not a special state reserved for monks or advanced practitioners. It is
a turn of the heart, simple and complete. *I wish to be free, and I wish to
help others be free.* From that turn, the path unfolds.

Two aspects are often named:

- **Aspiring bodhicitta:** the wish to help all beings become free from
  suffering. The seed, planted in the heart.

- **Engaging bodhicitta:** taking action through compassion, wisdom, and the
  path of practice. The seed, growing into a tree.

Together they transform spiritual practice from personal improvement into a
boundless commitment to the welfare of all.
